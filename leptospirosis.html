Considerations

  • Routine vaccination of shelter dogs with the quadrivalent Leptospirosis vaccine isnot advised. Even in communities with commonly reported cases of canine leptospirosis, the incidence of this disease in animal shelters is low.
  • Contaminated environments present the biggest ongoing risk to human health. Environmental management to reduce risk of exposure is more impactful in preventing infection; important mitigable factors include exposure to sewage, rodents, contaminated water, and livestock. Efforts to address these factors, including eliminating rodent infestations, appear to be more effective than vaccination in preventing leptospirosis in shelter dogs.
  • In shelters with demonstrated leptospirosis transmission within their facility, vaccination may be considered as part of a comprehensive disease mitigation strategy. In such a high-risk environment however, dogs are likely exposed before partial protection can develop following their second vaccine; leptospirosis vaccination does not help if given after exposure.
  • Risk of exposure varies geographically and is modified by individual lifestyle factors. Quiadrivalent leptospirosis vaccines should be consideredfor high-risk community dogs when resources allow and follow-up appointments for primary series revaccination are guaranteed. Annual revaccination is recommended after the primary series[{Updating}].

What the labels mean

Which vaccines

Core
Considered essential for all animals in that context category, and should be given at intake or as soon as possible. (Guideline definition)
Situational
May have good evidence for use under particular circumstances, but not appropriate for all shelter or community animals. (Guideline definition)

How certain the evidence is

Evidence rating
The task force's own rating of the evidence behind each recommendation, from 0 to 4, recorded when the recommendations were voted. Higher means more certain. A range means the statement rests on more than one voted recommendation and they were rated differently.
Not rated
No rating was recorded for this statement, usually because it was proposed after the vote.

Draws on the biology of: Severity, Onset of protection, Response to a single dose, Efficacy, Harms

Balance. Protection needs two doses and begins 1 to 2 weeks after the second, after most dogs have left, so the benefit during the stay is small. In-shelter incidence appears low, and dog-to-dog and dog-to-human transmission are rarely documented. Adverse events are no more common than with core vaccines, but cost and inventory complexity can divert resources from them. Favors not vaccinating routinely.

Basis. Inference from onset and length of stay, plus consensus on in-shelter incidence. Prevalence evidence is sparse and mostly Brazilian, and the one US study measured shedding rather than disease. Evidence on worker and zoonotic risk is observational and mostly from outside shelters.

Draws on the biology of: Onset of protection, Efficacy

Balance. Dogs that stay long enough to complete the series may gain partial protection against disease and shedding, but dogs exposed early will not be protected and vaccinating dogs does not protect staff. Environmental control addresses the source for dogs and people. Favors vaccination only as one part of a mitigation plan.

Basis. Consensus. The comparison of environmental control with vaccination rests on one confounded Brazilian study (Miotto 2018).

Draws on the biology of: Severity, Onset of protection, Duration of immunity, Efficacy, Harms

Balance. Vaccination gives partial protection against a severe zoonotic disease for dogs at real exposure risk, with adverse event rates similar to core vaccines. The benefit depends on completing the two-dose series and annual boosters, which many community clinic clients cannot return for, and on not displacing DAPP and rabies.

Basis. Evidence-based for efficacy in challenge trials (about 84% against disease, high risk of bias) and for identifiable risk factors. The follow-up and prioritization premises are consensus.
