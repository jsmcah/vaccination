Considerations

  • Routine vaccination of shelter and feral/free-roaming/community cats with the FeLV vaccine isnot advised. Transmission of FeLV in shelters is extremely rare, a single dose is not effective and most cats eligible for this vaccine will not remain in the shelter long enough to complete their primary series.
  • In sanctuary settings where young cats are group housed long-term and (ideally) FeLV testing is performed, this vaccine may be considered.
  • In community medicine clinics, this vaccine should be consideredfor high-risk cats when resources allow and follow-up appointments for primary series revaccination are guaranteed. Annual or biannual revaccination is recommended. For free-roaming cats, spay-neuter should be prioritized over vaccination in reducing risk factors for community transmission.

What the labels mean

Which vaccines

Core
Considered essential for all animals in that context category, and should be given at intake or as soon as possible. (Guideline definition)
Situational
May have good evidence for use under particular circumstances, but not appropriate for all shelter or community animals. (Guideline definition)

How certain the evidence is

Evidence rating
The task force's own rating of the evidence behind each recommendation, from 0 to 4, recorded when the recommendations were voted. Higher means more certain. A range means the statement rests on more than one voted recommendation and they were rated differently.
Not rated
No rating was recorded for this statement, usually because it was proposed after the vote.

Kinds of evidence

  • ■ Experimental
  • ● Observational
  • ▲ Case report or series
  • ◆ Expert or consensus
  • ■ filled: independent or not stated   □ hollow: manufacturer funded, authored, or supplied
  • (species) study in a different species
  • (model) decision model
  • Italic no study cited: Inference (reasoned from cited claims), Consensus (the task force's judgment), Not assembled (an evidence gap)

Limits of the evidence for this claim
Castillo-Aliaga 2026 classed cats by point-of-care antigen test and did not separate progressive from regressive infection, and its groups were small. Beall 2025 relied on owner-reported deaths. Hoover 1976 used intraperitoneal inoculation of SPF kittens. None of these sources measured immunosuppression directly
FeLV antigen positivity is more common in cats with bite wounds or abscesses, outdoor access, or intact males, which fits spread through close contact and fighting ●, ●
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Cross-sectional associations from point-of-care antigen tests without confirmation. They do not show the route of spread. Spread through saliva during grooming and from queen to kittens is not supported by any study in Zotero

ClaimCitation

Limits of the evidence for this claim
No study cited. The challenge studies reviewed challenged 2 to 3 weeks after the second dose (Jarrett 1996 at 2 weeks)

Duration of immunity

ClaimCitation
Protection lasted at least 2 years after an inactivated vaccine in one challenge study ■
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Not found in Zotero, so unverified. ABCD 2021 reports 10 of 12 vaccinated cats protected versus 0 of 11 controls (PF 83%)
Age resistance in control cats can make duration of immunity studies beyond 1 year hard to interpret ■
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Wilson 2012 is not in Zotero and was checked against the abstract only. Jirjis 2010, also not in Zotero, infected all 11 controls at 2 years, so age resistance does not undermine every long duration study

Limits of the evidence for this claim
Consensus documents. The intervals rest on few duration of immunity studies, most run by manufacturers

Response to a single dose

ClaimCitation
A single dose is not effective, and a two-dose primary series is required Consensus
Limits of the evidence for this claim
No study tests one dose. Efficacy studies challenge only after two doses, so single-dose efficacy is untested rather than shown to be absent
Guidelines give two doses 3 to 4 weeks apart, starting as early as 8 weeks ◇, ◆
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Consensus and product labels, not studies. The published table gives 2 to 4 weeks, shorter than every label and guideline in the documents
The series must be restarted if more than 6 weeks pass between doses Consensus
Limits of the evidence for this claim
No source cited. A member said on 8.20.25 that only manufacturers give this rule

Kittens and maternal antibody

ClaimCitation
Maternal antibody may protect kittens until about 16 weeks Not assembled
Limits of the evidence for this claim
The only source traced is Jarrett 1977, which is not in Zotero. As reported by ABCD 2021 it studied kittens of queens that recovered from infection, not vaccinated queens

Harms

ClaimCitation
Injection-site lymphomas expressing FeLV proteins have been reported, a reason to test before vaccinating ▲
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Only 5 of 17 cats had received FeLV vaccine, all had FVRCP, and there is no denominator
Adjuvanted FeLV vaccines raise concern for injection-site sarcoma Not assembled
Limits of the evidence for this claim
No primary study in Zotero. The case-control studies that exist link FeLV and rabies vaccination to injection-site sarcoma but do not isolate adjuvant as the cause
Whether FeLV vaccination affects antigen or PCR results in blood drawn soon after vaccination has not been studied Not assembled
Limits of the evidence for this claim
No primary study in Zotero tests FeLV antigen or PCR results after vaccination. ABCD 2021 cautions that blood drawn right after vaccination may contain vaccine antigen, citing the 2008 AAFP guideline, and says the duration of this is unknown

Age and susceptibility

ClaimCitation
Kittens are most susceptible, and resistance to progressive infection rises with age ■
Limits of the evidence for this claim
SPF cats inoculated intraperitoneally with two laboratory strains. Figures were checked against the PubMed abstract only, because the PDF is not in Zotero
Field prevalence studies do not clearly show infection concentrated in cats under 2 years ●, ●, ●
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Prevalence mixes age at infection with survival. Stavisky 2017 found infected shelter cats older than uninfected cats (median 4.75 versus 3.5 years), and De Almeida 2012 found highest risk at 1 to 5 years. Biezus 2023 has no PDF in Zotero

Draws on the biology of: Response to a single dose, Efficacy, Age and susceptibility

Balance. A single dose is not expected to protect, most cats leave before a second dose could take effect, and transmission is thought to be rare where cats are housed separately. Each dose costs about twice an FVRCP dose and ideally needs a prior test. Favors not vaccinating routinely in short-stay shelters.

Basis. Consensus and inference from the two-dose requirement and length of stay. No transmission or cost data were cited.

Draws on the biology of: Age and susceptibility, Efficacy, Duration of immunity

Balance. Young cats held for months in groups can complete the series and face repeated exposure as new cats arrive. Vaccines protected most challenged cats from progressive infection but not from regressive infection, so testing and separation of positive cats are still needed. Benefit falls as resistance rises with age.

Basis. Small experimental challenge studies, mostly manufacturer funded, and other guidelines. No sanctuary field data.
