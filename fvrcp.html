Considerations

  • Modified live FVRCP vaccinationis *strongly recommended *at or before intake for all shelter cats, at any age for community cats, and at the first encounter for community clinic animals. Shelter-housed kittens should receive a first vaccine by at least 4 weeks of age, but may be vaccinated earlier where risk of panleukopenia is meaningfully elevated. For kittens living in shelters or other high-risk environments, FVRCP should be repeated every 2 weeks until shelter exit or >20 weeks old. For kittens in low-risk foster or community homes, FVRCP can be started at 4-6 weeks, and repeated every 2-4 weeks until shelter exit or >20 weeks old. For adult cats, except where lengths of stay are >30 days and the risk of panleukopenia is meaningfully elevated, one vaccine dose is sufficient.

What the labels mean

Which vaccines

Core
Considered essential for all animals in that context category, and should be given at intake or as soon as possible. (Guideline definition)
Situational
May have good evidence for use under particular circumstances, but not appropriate for all shelter or community animals. (Guideline definition)

How certain the evidence is

Evidence rating
The task force's own rating of the evidence behind each recommendation, from 0 to 4, recorded when the recommendations were voted. Higher means more certain. A range means the statement rests on more than one voted recommendation and they were rated differently.
Not rated
No rating was recorded for this statement, usually because it was proposed after the vote.

Kinds of evidence

  • ■ Experimental
  • ● Observational
  • ▲ Case report or series
  • ◆ Expert or consensus
  • ■ filled: independent or not stated   □ hollow: manufacturer funded, authored, or supplied
  • (species) study in a different species
  • (model) decision model
  • Italic no study cited: Inference (reasoned from cited claims), Consensus (the task force's judgment), Not assembled (an evidence gap)

Feline herpesvirus 19 claims

Severity

ClaimCitation
FHV causes high morbidity and low mortality, with severe disease mostly in kittens and debilitated cats Consensus
Limits of the evidence for this claim
No citation is given for the morbidity and mortality statement.
Most infected cats become latent carriers that shed intermittently, often after stress ◆
Limits of the evidence for this claim
The source chapter is not in Zotero, so the figure of about 80% latent carriers could not be checked.

Efficacy

ClaimCitation
FHV vaccines reduce clinical signs and shedding but do not prevent infection □, ■, □, ■, ■, ■, □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
The challenge studies used two parenteral doses (Jas 2009) or IN vaccine (Cocker 1986, Lappin 2006). DiGangi 2011 and Dawson 2001 measured antibody only. Edinboro 1999 compared killed SQ vaccine with and without IN vaccine.
Whether vaccinating latently infected cats reduces reactivation or shedding is unknown Not assembled
Limits of the evidence for this claim
No study of parenteral vaccination in already infected cats.

Onset of protection

ClaimCitation
MLV herpes vaccine protects against challenge within 3 to 7 days ■, ■, □, □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
No study shows this for one SQ dose. In Reagan 2014, one SQ MLV dose 7 days before challenge did not reduce clinical signs compared with unvaccinated kittens. Lappin 2009 is antibody only, and 2 of 10 cats reached FHV titers above 8 by day 28.
IN FHV vaccine protects within 2 to 6 days ■, □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Small groups. Cocker 1986 had 4 cats per group, with partial protection at 2 days and near complete protection at 4 days. In Lappin 2006 the 2-day group did not differ significantly on its own.

Response to a single dose

ClaimCitation
Few cats seroconvert to FHV after one parenteral MLV dose □, ■
Limits of the evidence for this claim
2 of 11 SPF kittens seroconverted after the first dose and 7 of 11 after the second (Jas 2009). FHV antibody is a weak correlate of protection, and cell-mediated immunity matters.
Parenteral FHV vaccines were tested after two doses, so whether one dose suffices is unknown □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
This is an absence of single-dose data, not evidence that a second dose is needed. The second dose is usually given before antibody from the first would have risen.

Previously vaccinated animals

ClaimCitation
Previously vaccinated adult cats rarely show an FHV titer rise after a booster ○
Limits of the evidence for this claim
9 of 109 (8.3%, 95% CI 4.2 to 15.1) had a four-fold rise, and 40.9% had antibody before vaccination. Antibody only, 28-day follow-up.

Kittens and maternal antibody

ClaimCitation
Maternal antibody blocks FHV vaccine responses in some kittens until 17 weeks or later ■
Limits of the evidence for this claim
With inactivated vaccine, 0 of 3 kittens with high and 7 of 8 with low maternal antibody had protective titers at 14 and 17 weeks. 44% of all 27 kittens lacked FHV titers at 17 weeks. Antibody only.
Kittens vaccinated from 6 weeks responded as well as those started at 9 weeks ●
Limits of the evidence for this claim
At 9 weeks, 9 of 20 early and 1 of 20 conventional kittens had FHV antibody, with no difference at 12 and 15 weeks. Antibody only, and 68 of 137 kittens completed.
Maternal antibody in kittens is lowest at 3 to 4 weeks, leaving many unprotected by 4 weeks ■, ◇
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Casal 1996 measured total immunoglobulin in 46 kittens from 6 queens, not FHV-specific antibody.
Repeat MLV doses less than 2 weeks apart may interfere with each other ◆, ◇
Limits of the evidence for this claim
No feline FHV data. Sykes 2013 says successive live vaccines at 3 to 14 day intervals may interfere and points to human practice.
IN vaccine works despite maternal antibody ■, □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Reagan 2014 used FHV-naive kittens without maternal antibody, so it does not address this. The Zotero abstract of Johnson 1985 reports satisfactory protection in kittens with maternal antibody for both IN and killed parenteral vaccine.

Route

ClaimCitation
IN and SQ FHV vaccines have similar efficacy ■, ■
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Lappin 2009 compared antibody only, with 5 cats per group and few FHV responders. Johnson 1985 compared IN MLV with killed parenteral vaccine and has no PDF in Zotero.
Giving IN with SQ MLV vaccine may add protection over SQ alone □, □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Reagan 2014 used 8 naive kittens per group challenged 1 week after vaccination. Edinboro 1999 used killed, not MLV, SQ vaccine, lost 33 of 90 cats to follow-up and did not identify pathogens.
IN vaccination adds local and non-specific immunity that may protect against other pathogens ■
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Not in Zotero, so not checked.

Feline calicivirus 23 claims

Severity

ClaimCitation
FCV causes oral ulcers and respiratory signs, and some recovered cats shed virus for months or longer. ◇, ◆
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Consensus and review statements. No figures on how often FCV causes disease in US shelter cats.
VS-FCV outbreaks are rare but severe, with about 40% case fatality and vaccinated cats affected. ●, ◆
Limits of the evidence for this claim
One outbreak with no unvaccinated comparison. Hurley 2003 gives figures from unpublished data.
FCV vaccines reduce disease but do not prevent infection or shedding. ◆, ◇, ◆
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Reviews by FCV researchers. Radford 2006 judges current vaccines generally safe and effective against classic disease, a more favorable tone than the task force documents.

Strain coverage

ClaimCitation
FCV mutates rapidly, so circulating field strains often differ antigenically from vaccine strains. ◆, □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Poulet 2000 used infection antisera from 2 kittens per isolate and vaccinated no cats. It shows antigenic diversity, not vaccine failure.
Antisera to the F9 strain neutralized about a quarter of field isolates, against about four fifths for dual-strain antigens. □, □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Sera came from infected, not vaccinated, cats. Neutralization does not predict protection. Both studies were authored or funded by the dual-strain vaccine maker.
FCV isolated from vaccinated cats differed from the vaccine strains, and genogroup mix differed by vaccine strain. △
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Only 15 isolates and no denominator of vaccinated cats that stayed well, so susceptibility cannot be inferred.
MLV F9 vaccine induced cellular but not neutralizing immunity against current field strains. □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Five cats per group, many immunologic outcomes tested without correction.
Dual-strain or newer-strain vaccines protected against VS-FCV challenge better than F9 or no vaccine. □, □, □, □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
All four were run by vaccine makers on their own products. Huang 2010 and Cao 2023 challenged only with strains in the vaccine, and their single-strain comparison groups were not challenged.

Onset of protection

ClaimCitation
Inactivated dual-strain vaccine reduced signs at challenge 1 and 4 weeks after the second of two doses. □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Merial authored. All vaccinates still shed. Gives onset after the second dose of an inactivated product, not after one MLV dose.
Inactivated FCV vaccines need two doses, and one inactivated dose did not protect. □, □, ◇
Limits of the evidence for this claim
One small manufacturer study tested a single inactivated dose. No study tests the restart rule.
MLV vaccine raised FCV titers sooner than inactivated vaccine in kittens with low maternal antibody. ■, □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Antibody outcome, and FCV titers predict protection poorly. Lappin 2012 found no significant FCV difference between vaccines, and inactivated titers fell below the cutoff in 5 of 6 kittens by day 42.

Duration of immunity

ClaimCitation
Inactivated FCV vaccine gave partial protection 3 to 7.5 years later without reducing shedding. ■, □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Small groups of SPF cats with one inactivated product each. Jas 2015 was Merial authored.

Response to a single dose

ClaimCitation
One SQ dose of an MLV F9 combination vaccine reduced signs and shedding after heterologous challenge at 3 weeks. □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Virbac authored and unblinded, 10 kittens per group. All cats shed virus, half the vaccinates still had signs, and the FeLV fraction was adjuvanted.
No study compares one with two MLV FCV doses in adult cats, and licensing studies test two doses. □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Brunner 2005 compares MLV with and without FeLV vaccine and reports group median titers only, with no one versus two dose comparison.
Cats given one IN MLV dose resisted FCV challenge 11 months later. □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Heska funded and authored, and Heska made the IN vaccine. All vaccinated cats were seropositive and resistant, so the study cannot test whether one dose fails.

Kittens and maternal antibody

ClaimCitation
FCV maternal antibody wanes to undetectable levels between 10 and 14 weeks of age. □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
12 kittens of 4 queens immune from experimental infection, not vaccination.
One of 11 kittens with high maternal antibody lacked a protective FCV titer at 17 weeks. ■
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Kittens of SPF queens vaccinated with inactivated product, so maternal antibody may be lower than in the field. Antibody outcome.
Starting at 6 weeks gave earlier FCV responses in seronegative kittens but similar responses by 12 to 15 weeks. ●
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Only 52% to 59% of kittens showed an FCV response by 15 weeks. Intervet authored and part funded.

Route

ClaimCitation
One IN MLV dose raised FCV titers sooner than one SQ dose. ■
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Ten cats, no challenge. The difference was significant on days 10 and 14 only, and all cats were positive by day 28.
IN FVRCP given 4 to 6 days before FHV challenge reduced signs, but 2 days did not. □
Limits of the evidence for this claim
FHV challenge only, so it gives no FCV efficacy data. Heska funded and authored.
Adding IN MLV FHV/FCV to killed SQ vaccine at intake lowered URI in one shelter trial. □, ●
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Alternating allocation, no blinding, 33 of 90 cats lost to follow-up, and the SQ comparator was killed vaccine, not MLV. Crude RR 0.56 (95% CI 0.29 to 1.08) crosses the null.

Harms

ClaimCitation
Shelters that gave IN FVRC vaccine at intake had more adult URI. ●
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Shelter-level exposure in 9 shelters (4 using IN), close to an ecological comparison. Vaccine reactions cannot be separated from infection.
Harms of SQ MLV FCV vaccine, such as vaccine-strain limping, have not been assembled. ◆
Limits of the evidence for this claim
Radford 2006 notes occasional vaccine-induced disease with live vaccines but gives no rates.

Draws on the biology of: Severity, Onset of protection, Harms

Balance. FPV kills 50% to 90% of affected cats, many incoming cats are unprotected, and one MLV dose protects within days. Harms of one dose have not been assembled but are expected to be small. Favors vaccination at intake in nearly every shelter.

Basis. Evidence-based for biology and onset. The timing claim (intake versus later) is inferred.
